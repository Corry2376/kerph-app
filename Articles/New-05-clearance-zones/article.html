Here's the mistake, and almost everyone makes it once: you measure the table saw, you measure the wall, you do the arithmetic, and the saw fits. Then you get it home and discover you can't rip a sheet of plywood because the infeed run puts you inside the water heater.

The footprint of a machine is the least useful number on the spec sheet. What decides whether a shop actually works is the clearance zone — the space around each tool that has to stay empty for the tool to do its job. And for most machines, that zone is several times the size of the machine itself.

Four Different Kinds of Clearance

They're not the same thing and they don't behave the same way, which is why a single "leave three feet around everything" rule fails.

Material clearance is space for the workpiece, not the machine. A table saw ripping an eight-foot board needs eight feet in front and eight feet behind. The saw is 30 inches wide. The zone is over sixteen feet long. This is the one that surprises people and it dominates every other consideration in a small shop.

Operator clearance is where your body has to be to work safely — standing position, room to shift your weight, room to step back out of the line of a kickback rather than into a wall.

Service clearance is access for blade changes, belt changes, dust port cleanouts, and getting a wrench onto the thing that needs a wrench. Skip this and you'll be dragging a 400-pound saw into the middle of the floor twice a year.

Walkway clearance is the path between everything else. Thirty-six inches is the comfortable minimum for a main path. Thirty is workable. Twenty-four is where you start turning sideways to pass and carrying boards becomes a two-step maneuver.

What Each Machine Actually Needs

These are working numbers for a home shop, not code minimums.

Table saw. The demanding one, by a mile. Full-length ripping wants 8 feet infeed and 8 feet outfeed. Rip capacity to the right of the blade plus about 24 inches of standing room to the left. In practice almost nobody has that, and the standard compromise is an outfeed table that doubles as an assembly bench, plus positioning the saw so long stock can run out an open garage door on the days you need it. That last trick is genuinely how most one-car shops handle sheet goods.

Jointer. Infeed and outfeed equal to your longest board, so 6 feet each way for typical stock. Access along the full front. Almost nothing needed behind it, which is why jointers live against walls.

Planer. Same as the jointer — 6 feet each end. If it's on a stand near the saw, it can borrow the saw's outfeed space as long as they don't need it simultaneously. They rarely do.

Miter saw. The one people under-plan. Cutting an eight-foot trim board means 4 feet of support on each side of the blade, so a miter station is realistically 10 feet of wall. A saw on a 3-foot bench with nothing beside it can only safely cut short pieces.

Bandsaw. Modest. About 3 feet in front, and enough on the left for the workpiece to swing while you steer a curve. Needs real service clearance on the wheel-cover side for blade changes.

Drill press. Small footprint, but you need enough room for the workpiece to rotate if the bit grabs it, and clearance above for tall pieces if the head is near a low ceiling or joist.

Workbench. Three feet on the working side minimum. If you're planing by hand, you want room to move along its length rather than standing still.

Dust collector. Footprint plus room to swap the bag without wrestling it. Bags need vertical clearance to lift free — check that against your ceiling before you tuck one under a low soffit.

The Trick That Makes It All Fit: Zones Overlap

This is the insight that turns an impossible layout into a workable one, and it's why "add up all the clearances" gives you a number that says your shop is too small when it isn't.

Clearance zones can share space, as long as you're not using both tools at once. And you're never using both tools at once, because there's one of you.

The table saw's outfeed area can be the assembly table. The jointer's infeed run can cross the planer's outfeed run. The miter saw's left-hand support can be the workbench. A one-car garage that would need 600 square feet if every zone were exclusive works fine in 240 because the zones stack.

What can't overlap is anything permanent. A zone can share space with another zone. It cannot share space with a lumber rack, a water heater, a floor drain, a support post, or the car that has to fit back in at the end of the night.

Rotation Changes Everything

The part that's genuinely hard to do in your head: a machine at an angle has a different clearance shape than the same machine square to the wall, and it's not a simple scaling. Turn a table saw 30 degrees to open up a diagonal rip path and the zone rotates with it — sweeping across floor that was clear a moment ago and freeing up floor that wasn't.

Diagonal layouts are genuinely powerful in square rooms, because the longest straight line in any rectangle is its diagonal. A 20x20 garage has a 28-foot diagonal. That's the difference between ripping 8-foot stock and not.

It's also where graph paper falls apart, because you're now doing trigonometry to find out whether a rotated rectangle overlaps a fixed obstacle. That's exactly what the Workshop Layout Planner is for — it draws each tool's real clearance zone, rotates the zone with the tool, and flags the collision when a zone lands on something it shouldn't. You find out on screen instead of finding out with a board in your hands.

The Test That Tells You If a Layout Works

Before you move a single machine, walk the four operations you actually do most and ask where the wood goes:

Breaking down a sheet of plywood. Where does the sheet enter, where does it get cut, where do the offcuts land? If the answer involves lifting a full sheet over a machine, the layout is wrong.

Milling a rough board. Jointer, planer, saw, in that order. Is that a walk or a relay race? Each handoff should be a step or two, not a lap.

Crosscutting trim. Is there support on both sides of the miter saw, or are you holding an eight-foot board cantilevered off a bench and hoping?

Assembly. Is there a flat surface big enough for the largest thing you build, that isn't also the outfeed table you need clear at the same time?

If all four have a clean answer, the layout works. If one of them ends in "well, I'd move the saw first," that's not a layout — that's a chore you've agreed to do forever.

The Short Version

Measure the wood, not the machine. Let zones overlap, because you're one person. Never let a zone overlap something that can't move. Walk your four common operations before you commit.

And spend the hour planning it on paper or on screen. Moving a 400-pound cabinet saw twice because the first spot didn't work is a genuinely bad afternoon, and it's entirely avoidable.

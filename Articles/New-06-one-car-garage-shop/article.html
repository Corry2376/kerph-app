A single-car garage is about 12 by 20 feet. Call it 240 square feet, minus the water heater, minus whatever's on the walls, minus the bikes. It is a genuinely small space for woodworking, and most of the advice written about it is either fantasy or a thinly disguised ad for a tool you can't afford.

So let's be honest about the constraints first, because working with them is the whole trick.

The Three Constraints That Decide Everything

The garage door is your best feature. Not a limitation — an asset, and probably the single most valuable thing in the room. It's a 16-foot-long clearance zone that costs you nothing and disappears when you close it. Almost every workable one-car layout is built around using that opening for long stock. Point your saw at it.

The car is a real question, and you have to answer it honestly. "The car still parks here" and "this is a shop" are two different rooms. A shared garage means every machine is mobile, nothing is permanently plumbed, and you lose 20 minutes at each end of every session rolling things around. That's a real cost, paid every single time. Some people happily pay it for years. Some people quietly stop going out to the shop and never quite admit why. Decide which one you are before you buy a mobile base for everything.

You will never have enough outfeed. Accept it and design around it rather than fighting it. Every good small-shop layout is a set of clever answers to "where does the long board go."

Start With the Long Axis

The longest usable line in a 12x20 garage isn't 20 feet — it's the diagonal, about 23 feet, and if the door's open it's effectively unlimited out the front.

That gives you two viable strategies:

Door-axis layout. Table saw positioned so ripping runs straight out the open door. Simple, effective, and it means you can handle 8-foot stock any day the weather allows. The catch is that it's weather-dependent, and in February in a cold climate "open the garage door" is not a casual decision.

Diagonal layout. Saw rotated 20 to 30 degrees so the rip path runs corner to corner inside the room. Buys you real length with the door closed. Costs you tidiness — a diagonal machine wastes triangles of floor around it, and those triangles are only useful for things that don't need to be square to anything.

Most working one-car shops end up as a hybrid: everything against walls, one machine on a mobile base that rolls into a diagonal position when a long rip comes up, and the door available on good days.

What Goes Where

Along the long wall: the miter saw station and workbench. Both want length, both are happy against a wall, and both can share support surfaces. A 10-foot run gives you a miter saw with 4 feet of support each side. Put the bench directly beside it at the same height and the bench becomes the left-hand support. Two functions, one surface.

Center or near-center, mobile: the table saw. It needs access on multiple sides and it needs to move, so it's the one machine that shouldn't live against a wall. On a good mobile base it rolls out for use and tucks back for parking.

Short wall, opposite the door: jointer and planer, in line with each other, feeding along the wall. This is the arrangement that makes a small shop work — a jointer and planer set up in line share one infeed run and one outfeed run instead of needing four. Mill a board on the jointer, pivot, run it through the planer, same floor space.

Corners and vertical: dust collector, air compressor, lumber storage. Corners are terrible for machines that need access on multiple sides and perfect for things you approach from one direction.

Overhead: sheet goods on a ceiling rack, clamps on the walls, lighting. The cubic footage above 6 feet is the most under-used space in nearly every garage shop.

Mobile Bases: Which Tools Actually Need to Roll

The instinct is to put everything on wheels. Resist it — mobile bases cost money, add height (which matters when you're trying to match outfeed heights), and introduce a small amount of wobble to a machine you'd rather have planted.

Genuinely needs to be mobile: the table saw, because it needs multi-side access and it's the machine most likely to want repositioning for a long cut. The planer, if it's a lunchbox that lives on a shelf and comes out to work.

Usually fine stationary: the jointer, drill press, bandsaw, and workbench. These are approached from one side, they live against a wall, and they don't need to move. A bandsaw is tall and narrow and genuinely more pleasant to use when it isn't on casters.

Depends entirely on the car: everything, if the car comes back every night. That's the cost of a shared garage and it's worth naming plainly.

If you do buy bases, get ones rated well above the machine's weight and with locking casters that actually lock. A decent universal mobile base is one of those purchases where the cheap version is genuinely worse in a way you feel every time.

The Things People Forget Until It's Too Late

Electrical. A one-car garage typically has one or two 15-amp circuits, frequently shared with outdoor outlets. A table saw and a dust collector running together will trip that. Plan for it: either run a second circuit, or wire the shop so the collector and the saw are on different circuits, or accept that you'll be resetting a breaker. If you're adding 220V for a bigger saw, sort that out before the layout is fixed, because the outlet location constrains where the machine can live.

Ceiling height and the door track. The rails for the garage door eat the center of your ceiling. Overhead storage and dust ducting have to route around them, and a ceiling-mounted air cleaner may not fit where you assumed.

The floor. Garage floors slope toward the door for drainage — often a quarter inch per foot. Over 20 feet that's five inches, which is enough to matter when you're trying to get an outfeed table level with a saw. Shims are part of the plan, not an afterthought.

Heat and cold. An uninsulated garage in a cold climate is a shop you use four months a year. Insulation is boring and it's probably a better investment than your next tool.

Do It On Paper First

Everything above is general. Your garage has a water heater in a specific corner, a door that swings a specific way, and a post you're not allowed to remove.

Draw it to scale before you move anything. Include the immovable things — that's the part people leave off, and it's the part that ruins layouts. Then place machines with their real clearance zones and see what collides.

That's what the Workshop Layout Planner does: your actual dimensions, your actual tools with their real footprints and clearance zones, walls and doors and outlets where they really are. The Honest Summary

A one-car garage makes a real woodshop. It doesn't make a comfortable one, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

Point your longest cut at the door. Put the jointer and planer in line. Make the saw mobile and leave the rest planted. Use the vertical space. Be honest about the car.

Do that, and you've got a shop that works. Not a shop from a magazine. A shop that builds things, which is the only kind that matters.

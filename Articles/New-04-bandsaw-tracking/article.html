"My bandsaw won't track straight" describes three completely different problems, and people routinely try to fix one by adjusting for another. So before you touch a knob, sort out which one you actually have:

Tracking is where the blade rides on the wheels. If it wanders toward the edge or falls off, that's tracking.

Drift is the blade cutting at a slight angle to the fence, so ripping a straight line requires feeding at a skew. The blade stays on the wheels fine. It just doesn't cut where you point it.

Deflection is the blade bending mid-cut — usually a barrel-shaped resaw cut, fat in the middle.

Same complaint, three causes. Here's how to sort each one.

First: Tension, Because Everything Else Depends On It

Under-tensioning is the single most common bandsaw problem in home shops, and it causes symptoms in all three categories. Blade won't track. Blade drifts. Blade deflects and barrels in a resaw. It also breaks blades early, which feels like the opposite of what under-tension should do until you think about how much a loose blade flexes on every revolution.

Here's the honest problem: the tension scale on your saw is probably lying to you. Those gauges are calibrated at the factory for a specific blade, they drift as the spring ages, and on many consumer saws they were never accurate to begin with. Setting a 1/2" blade to the "1/2 inch" mark is a starting point, not an answer.

The practical method: tension it, then deflect the blade sideways with a finger halfway between the guides with the guides backed off. You want roughly 1/4" of deflection under moderate pressure. Less than that and it's too tight; noticeably more and it's too loose. It's a feel you develop, and it's more reliable than the gauge on most saws.

Most home shops need to go one to two marks past what the scale says. That's not me telling you to over-tension — it's telling you the scale reads low, which is the common failure. And release the tension when the saw sits for weeks. It saves the tires and the wheel bearings.

Tracking: Getting the Blade to Stay Put

Tracking is controlled by the tilt of the upper wheel, and almost nothing else. There's a knob on the back of the saw that tilts it.

The process, and do it with the saw unplugged while spinning the wheel by hand:

1. Back the guides completely out of the way. Both the side guides and the thrust bearings, top and bottom. Guides are not tracking devices. If a guide is touching the blade while you set tracking, you're setting tracking against the guide, which is not a thing that works.

2. Tension the blade properly first. Tracking changes with tension. Set tension, then track.

3. Spin the upper wheel by hand and adjust the tilt knob until the blade settles where you want it and stays there through several full revolutions.

Where should it sit? On a saw with crowned rubber tires, the blade wants to ride with the gullets — the valleys between the teeth — at or near the crown of the tire. On flat tires, center the blade body on the tire. The one rule that holds everywhere: no part of the tooth set should be riding on the tire. Teeth on rubber chews the tire up fast and it's an expensive mistake to leave running.

4. Then set the guides. Side guides just behind the gullets, close but not pinching — a dollar bill's thickness of clearance is the classic gauge and it's a good one. Thrust bearings about a business card's width behind the blade, so they're not spinning until you actually feed stock.

When Tracking Won't Hold No Matter What You Do

If the blade tracks fine, then wanders after a minute, or refuses to settle anywhere, you have a mechanical problem rather than an adjustment problem. In rough order of likelihood:

Dirty or worn tires. Pitch and resin build up on the rubber and change the crown profile the blade rides on. Clean them with a stiff brush and mineral spirits. If the rubber is cracked, hardened, or has a groove worn in it, it needs replacing — and a groove is a guarantee the blade will keep falling into it.

Wheels out of coplanar. The two wheels need to be in the same plane. Lay a long straightedge across both wheel rims — a level works — and look at the gaps. Shims behind the upper wheel correct it on many saws. This is a real and common problem on inexpensive saws and it drives people to distraction because every adjustment "almost" works.

A bad weld on the blade. Watch the blade through a full revolution. If it kicks at the same spot every time, that spot is the weld. A blade with a bad or misaligned weld will never track cleanly and no adjustment fixes it. Replace the blade.

A blade with a set problem — teeth set more aggressively on one side pull the blade that way. Again: replace it.

Drift: The One That Isn't Really a Defect

Drift is when the blade cuts at a slight angle, so you have to feed the board at a skew to rip straight. It's the thing that makes people fight their fence.

Here's the shift in thinking that helps: on many saws, a small amount of drift is a property of the blade, not a fault in the machine. Different blades on the same saw drift different amounts and different directions.

To measure it: draw a straight line down a wide board, freehand-cut along that line without the fence, and when you're a foot in, stop and hold the board still. Mark the board's angle relative to the miter slot or table edge. That angle is your drift for that blade.

Then you have two options. Set your fence to that angle — most aftermarket resaw fences let you do exactly this — or reduce the drift at the source: increase tension, adjust guides properly, and use a sharp blade. Under-tension is again the biggest single contributor, which is why this article started there.

A well-tensioned, sharp, properly guided blade on a coplanar saw usually drifts little enough to ignore. If yours drifts a lot, that's a symptom pointing back up this article, not a fence problem.

Blade Choice Solves More Than You'd Think

A dull blade wanders. A too-wide blade fights curves. A too-narrow blade deflects in a resaw. Wrong tooth count packs with sawdust and heats up.

Rough guide: 1/4" for curves, 1/2" as the do-everything general blade, 3/4" or wider with 3 TPI for resawing. For resaw work specifically, a low-tooth-count resaw blade makes a bigger difference than any adjustment on this list — you want big gullets to clear the enormous volume of dust a deep cut generates. Fine-tooth blades in thick stock is how you get a burned, wandering cut that looks like a tracking problem and isn't.

The Order to Work In

Every time, in this order, and don't skip ahead:

1. Is the blade sharp and undamaged? If not, nothing below matters.
2. Tension it properly — assume the gauge reads low.
3. Back the guides off entirely.
4. Set tracking with the upper wheel tilt, spinning by hand.
5. Set side guides and thrust bearings.
6. Only now, test cut and evaluate drift.

Nearly every bandsaw complaint I've seen resolves somewhere in steps 1 through 4, and most of those in step 2. The saw is usually fine. It's usually the tension, and it's usually the gauge that convinced you the tension was already right.

The simple answer is that most woodworking machines want somewhere between 350 and 800 CFM at the tool, and a decent single-stage collector is rated for more than that. The real answer is that the rating on the box is measured with no ducting attached, and by the time the air has traveled through 30 feet of hose, three fittings and a blast gate, you may be delivering less than half of it.

That gap is why so many shops have a "good" dust collector and a fine dust problem.

Two Numbers, and People Confuse Them Constantly

CFM is volume — cubic feet of air per minute. It's what carries chips through the duct.

Static pressure is resistance — how hard the system fights back, measured in inches of water. Every foot of duct, every elbow, every flex hose adds to it.

These trade off against each other. A blower producing 1,200 CFM against zero resistance might produce 600 CFM against six inches of static pressure. The manufacturer prints the first number. Your shop delivers the second.

This is why "my collector is rated 1,200 CFM, so I'm fine" is one of the most common wrong conclusions in shop setup. The rating isn't a lie. It's just measured in a condition your shop will never be in.

What Each Machine Actually Wants

Working targets, measured at the tool port:

Table saw — 350 to 400 CFM through the cabinet port. Add an over-blade guard collector and you want another 300, because the dust thrown up above the table is the fine stuff and the cabinet port never sees it.

Planer — 400 to 500. Planers produce more chip volume per minute than anything else in a home shop. This is about moving material, not just capturing dust.

Jointer — 350 to 400.

Bandsaw — 350 to 400. Bandsaws are notoriously bad at presenting dust to a port, so real-world capture is poor regardless.

Router table — 350 at the fence, plus below-table collection if you have it. Routers make a lot of very fine dust, which is the kind that matters most for your lungs.

Miter saw — genuinely hopeless. The blade throws dust in an arc no hood catches well. 500+ CFM and a good hood helps; nothing makes it clean.

Drum sander — 500 to 600, and non-negotiable, because a clogged sander burns the workpiece and the belt.

Lathe — 400+, positioned close, and it still misses most of it.

Chips Versus Fine Dust

Two different problems, and the one you can see is the less dangerous one.

Chips are the visible stuff. Annoying, a slip hazard, and they clog machines. Air volume moves them.

Fine dust — under 10 microns, and especially under 5 — is what stays airborne for hours and reaches deep into your lungs. It's invisible in normal light. You can have a spotless floor and terrible air.

This matters for equipment choice: a shop vac has high static pressure and low CFM, which is great for a sander port and useless for a planer. A dust collector has high CFM and low static pressure, which is the opposite. And the filter matters as much as the motor — a 30-micron bag, which is what many collectors ship with, catches chips and blows fine dust straight back into the room through the bag's own weave. Upgrading to a 1-micron or better cartridge filter is often the highest-value change you can make to an existing collector, and it costs a fraction of a new machine.

Where Your CFM Actually Goes

This is the part that decides whether your system works, and it's almost entirely about ducting.

Flex hose is the biggest thief. Corrugated flex has roughly three times the resistance of smooth-wall pipe per foot. Ten feet of 4" flex can cost you more than thirty feet of smooth 4" duct. Use flex only for the last few feet where a machine needs to move, and keep those runs as short as you can stand.

Diameter matters enormously. Airflow scales with the square of the radius. Going from 4" to 6" duct roughly doubles the air you can move at the same pressure. If you're running a main trunk line, run it at 6" and reduce to 4" only at the machine.

Every elbow costs. A sharp 90-degree elbow is equivalent to several feet of straight pipe. Two gentle 45s beat one hard 90 every time.

Reducers and mismatched ports choke the whole run. A 6" main necked down to a 2.5" machine port means that branch is a 2.5" system no matter what's upstream.

Long runs. Resistance accumulates. The machine at the far end of a 40-foot run is not getting what the machine 8 feet away gets.

Sizing the Collector

The practical approach for a home shop:

1. Find your hungriest machine. Usually the planer or drum sander. That's your target at the tool — call it 500 CFM.

2. Assume real-world losses. A typical home shop with reasonable ducting loses 30 to 50 percent between the collector and the tool. To deliver 500, you want a machine rated around 800 to 1,000 CFM.

3. Only one machine runs at a time. This is the thing that keeps home dust collection affordable — you don't size for the sum of every port. You size for the biggest single one, and you use blast gates to send all the air there.

4. Buy the filter, not just the motor. A 1,200 CFM collector with a 30-micron bag is worse for your lungs than an 800 CFM unit with a 1-micron cartridge.

For most one- and two-car garage shops, a 1.5 HP single-stage collector with a good filter and short, mostly-rigid ducting is the sweet spot. Bigger motors don't fix bad ducting — they just make the same mistakes louder.

Do the Layout Before You Buy the Pipe

Duct length and elbow count are decided by where your machines sit, which means your dust collection performance is determined by your floor plan, and that's a sentence worth sitting with. Deciding the layout first and the ducting second is how people end up with a 40-foot run to the planer.

Put the collector centrally rather than in the far corner — it feels tidier in the corner and it costs you real airflow. Group the hungry machines nearest to it. Keep the trunk line short and let the branches be short too.

The Short Version

Size for your hungriest single machine, not the sum. Assume you'll lose a third to half of the rated CFM to ducting. Spend on the filter, because that's the part that protects your lungs rather than your floor. Use rigid pipe, run 6" where you can, and keep flex hose to the last few feet.

And plan the ducting when you plan the layout, not after. The pipe follows the machines, and by the time the machines are placed, most of your CFM decisions have already been made for you.

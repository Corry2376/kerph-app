A blast gate is a sliding door in a duct. Open, air flows. Closed, it doesn't. That's the whole concept, and it takes about four seconds to understand.

So why write about them? Because the concept is trivial and the execution is where dust collection systems quietly fail. Gates get mounted so they fill with sawdust and jam. They get placed where you can't reach them. They leak enough to starve the machine you're actually using. And a lot of people run systems with every gate open all the time, which is the same as having no gates at all — except you paid for them.

Why You Need Them At All

Your dust collector moves a fixed amount of air. If that air has five open paths to choose from, it splits between them, and every machine gets a fraction of what it needs.

Open branches don't just divide the flow — they take more than their share, because air is lazy and follows the path of least resistance. An open 6" branch running to nothing will happily take most of your airflow while the 4" branch at the machine you're actually using gets the leftovers.

The whole job of a blast gate is to close every path except the one you're using, so the entire capacity of the collector goes to the tool that's running. Close all but one, and a modest collector performs like a much bigger one. Leave them all open and an expensive collector performs like a shop vac with ambition.

The Orientation Mistake Almost Everyone Makes

Here's the single most useful thing in this article, and it's the thing nobody tells you when you buy the gates.

A blast gate has a slot the blade slides in. That slot is a pocket. In a horizontal duct run with the gate mounted so the blade slides vertically down, that pocket sits at the bottom of the airstream and fills with sawdust. Every time. Within a few weeks the gate won't close fully, and you'll be prying it with a screwdriver and wondering why the "self-cleaning" gates you bought aren't.

Mount gates so the blade slides horizontally, or so the slot is at the top rather than the bottom. Then gravity works with you: debris falls past the slot instead of into it, and the gate keeps working.

If the run makes horizontal mounting awkward, the second-best option is a self-cleaning gate design where the slot is genuinely enclosed. Those exist and they're worth the extra money in a run you can't orient well. What you should not do is mount a cheap gate slot-down in a horizontal run and hope. That's a maintenance chore you're scheduling for yourself.

Where to Put Them

One per branch, at the branch. Put the gate where the branch leaves the main trunk, not down at the machine. Gate at the trunk means the entire branch is dead air when it's closed, so there's no long stub of open pipe bleeding pressure.

The counter-argument is reach — a gate at the trunk might be 8 feet up near the ceiling while you're standing at the machine. That's a real tradeoff and it's why some people put the gate at the machine instead, accepting a small loss for the convenience.

The good answer is a gate at the branch with a remote — a pull cord, a lever, or one of the automated systems that opens the gate when the tool switches on. The lazy-but-honest answer is a gate at the machine, which is worse aerodynamically and much better at actually getting used. A gate you can't reach is a gate you'll leave open, and an open gate is worse than no gate.

Don't gate the main trunk. Gates go on branches. A gate in the trunk line just adds resistance to everything.

Metal or Plastic

Plastic gates are cheap, light, and fine for a 4" home shop system. They're also the ones most prone to the jamming problem above, and the blades can flex enough to leak under pressure.

Metal gates seal better, last longer, and cost more. For a 6" main system, or for the two or three branches you use daily, they're worth it. There's no shame in a mixed system — metal gates on the machines you use constantly, plastic on the ones you touch twice a month.

Whichever you buy, check the actual inner diameter. Some 4" gates neck down to less than 4" internally, and a restriction at every branch is a slow leak of performance across the whole system.

Sealing, Which Is Where the Quiet Losses Live

A blast gate that's "closed" but leaking is stealing from the machine you're using, and it's invisible. You won't hear it, you won't see dust, and the only symptom is that everything works slightly worse than it should.

Seal every joint. Foil tape, silicone, or gasket material at each connection — not duct tape, which dries out and lets go in a year. Where the gate body meets the pipe is the usual culprit, along with the seam of the gate housing itself on cheaper units.

Here's a test worth doing once: close every gate, turn the collector on, and listen. You should hear the blower load up and go quieter as it fights against a sealed system. If it sounds exactly the same as with a gate open, you've got leaks big enough to matter, and hunting them down is a better use of an hour than most upgrades.

One caution: don't run a single-stage collector fully closed for long. The motor cools itself with the airflow, and starving it entirely isn't good for it. A few seconds to test is fine; a coffee break is not.

Building the Habit

The system only works if the gates get used, so make the routine unmissable:

Default closed. Every gate closed is the resting state. You open one, use the machine, close it before you walk away. Default-open means you'll leave three open by accident on a busy afternoon.

Label them. A paint pen on each gate body with the machine name. Sounds unnecessary until you're standing under six identical gates trying to remember which is the bandsaw.

Position matters more than you'd think. A gate at chest height beside the machine gets used. A gate above head height in the corner does not, no matter how good your intentions were on installation day.

Consider automation eventually. Systems that open the gate and start the collector when the tool switches on genuinely fix the habit problem, because they remove the habit. They're not cheap and they're not necessary. They are, if you're honest about how often you forget, the thing that finally makes the system work as designed.

Plan the Gates With the Ducting

Where gates go is decided by where the branches go, which is decided by where the machines sit. Work it out on the layout rather than in the aisle at the store with a cart full of fittings.

The Short Version

Mount them so the slot doesn't collect sawdust. Put them at the branch, close to something you can reach. Seal everything. Default to closed. Label them.

None of this is complicated. All of it is the difference between a dust collection system that performs like the machine you paid for and one that performs like a hopeful suggestion.

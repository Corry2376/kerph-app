The simple answer is: buy the planer first. The real answer is that the question is wrong, and understanding why is worth more to you than the recommendation. A jointer and a planer are not two versions of the same tool. They're two halves of one process, and each one is physically incapable of doing the other's job. Skip that and you'll spend a year wondering why your boards keep coming out smooth, uniform, and subtly banana-shaped.

What Each One Actually Does

A jointer makes one face flat, and one edge square to it. That's the entire job. It has a long, split table with a cutterhead between the halves, and you press the board down onto the outfeed side as you feed. It doesn't care what the other side of the board looks like. It's making a reference surface — one honest, flat face you can measure everything else from.

A planer makes the second face parallel to the first, and brings the board to a specific thickness. Board goes in, rollers grab it, cutterhead takes a bite off the top, board comes out thinner. Here's the part that trips people up: a planer copies. Feed it a board with a bow in it, and the rollers press that bow flat against the bed while the knives cut. Board springs back on the way out. Now you have a bowed board that's a uniform thickness. Congratulations.

That's the whole relationship. The jointer creates flat. The planer creates parallel and thickness. Neither one substitutes for the other, and every workaround you'll read about online is a way of faking the jointer's job with something else.

So Why Buy the Planer First?

Because you can fake a jointer. You can't fake a planer.

There are three genuinely workable ways to get a flat face without owning a jointer: a planer sled (a flat torsion box with the board shimmed on it, run through the planer), a router sled for wide slabs, or hand planes if you've got the patience and the arm. All three are real techniques that real people use. They're slower than a jointer, and that's the honest cost, but they work.

Now try to fake a planer. Your options are a drum sander that costs more than the planer did, or thicknessing an entire board by hand, which is a genuinely miserable way to spend a weekend. There's no cheap workaround. The planer does something no other tool in a home shop does affordably.

There's a second reason, and it's the one that actually decides it for most people: a lunchbox planer costs about a third of what a comparable jointer does. A DeWalt DW735 or a Wen 6552 gets you a genuinely capable 13" planer. A 6" jointer that isn't frustrating to use costs meaningfully more and eats about five feet of floor space along one wall. On a first-tool budget, that's not a close call.

The Case for Buying Neither (Yet)

Here's the part nobody selling tools wants to lead with: you might not need either one.

If you're buying S4S lumber from a home center — already surfaced on four sides, straight, ready to cut — you have no milling problem to solve. You're paying a premium per board foot for someone else to have done this work, and for a lot of projects that's a perfectly rational trade. A jointer and planer pay for themselves by letting you buy rough lumber at roughly half the price. If you're building two projects a year, that payback period is measured in decades.

So the real first question isn't jointer or planer. It's: am I buying rough lumber? If the answer is no, and you're not itching to start, put the money into a better saw, better dust collection, or lumber for the next three projects. That's not me being cute. That's the actual best use of the money.

What Size, When You Do Buy

Planer: 13" is the sweet spot and has been for twenty years. The 12" models save you almost nothing and cost you the ability to surface a glued-up panel. Three-knife heads leave a better finish than two. A spiral or helical cutterhead is genuinely quieter and handles figured wood without tearout — it's the single best upgrade available on a lunchbox planer, and also the most expensive. Buy it later if the budget's tight; the machine works fine without it.

Jointer: 6" is where most people land, and most people eventually wish they'd gone 8". Here's the honest math: a 6" jointer handles the majority of what you'll actually buy, because most rough boards are under 6" wide or get ripped narrower anyway. An 8" handles more, costs substantially more, and weighs enough that it's a permanent installation rather than something you shuffle around. If you have the space and the money, 8". If you're deciding between a 6" jointer now and an 8" jointer in two years, buy the 6" now. Two years of flat boards beats two years of waiting.

The Space Problem Nobody Mentions

Both of these are long tools that need clear space on both ends, and that requirement is what actually kills them in a small shop. A 6" jointer with 46" of table needs roughly six feet of clear run on the infeed side and another six on the outfeed to handle an eight-foot board. A planer needs about the same. That's not floor space for the machine — that's floor space for the board, and it's the thing people forget until the day they're feeding a board into a wall.

The good news is you can share it. A jointer and planer positioned to feed along the same axis can use the same clear run, and if you set them up in line with a garage door, you can open the door and use the driveway as your infeed on the rare day you're milling ten-footers. That's exactly the kind of thing worth working out on paper before you buy — the Workshop Layout Planner shows you each machine's real clearance zones so you can find out whether the run actually fits before a 300-pound jointer is sitting in your garage.

The Order That Actually Works

If you're buying rough lumber and building toward a full milling setup, here's the sequence that gets you working fastest for the least money:

1. Planer first. Cheapest of the two, does the thing you can't fake, and immediately lets you buy rough lumber and get it to thickness.

2. Build a planer sled. An afternoon and a sheet of MDF. This is your jointer stand-in, and it's good enough that some people never move past it.

3. Jointer when the sled starts annoying you. And it will. The sled works, but it's setup time on every board, and one day you'll realize you've spent more hours shimming boards than the jointer would have cost in hourly wages. That's your signal.

Buy the planer. Build the sled. Use both until the sled makes you angry. That's when you buy the jointer, and by then you'll know exactly what size you need, because you'll know what you actually mill.

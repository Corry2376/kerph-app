A jointer's only job is to make one face flat. Everything after that — the planer, the table saw, the joinery — references that one flat face. Which means if you don't own a jointer, you're not missing a machine, you're missing a reference surface. And there are several ways to make one.

This matters more than it sounds, because a planer without a flat reference face doesn't fix a bowed board. The rollers press the bow flat, the knives cut, the board springs back on the way out, and you get a uniformly thick banana. Get the reference face right and the planer does the rest.

First: Learn to See What's Actually Wrong

Boards go out of flat in four ways, and the fix depends on which you've got:

Cup — curved across the width, like a shallow trough. Extremely common in flatsawn boards.

Bow — curved along the length, face up.

Crook — curved along the length, edge-on. Sometimes called spring.

Twist — the two ends are in different planes. The worst one, and the hardest to see.

Cup and bow you can spot by laying the board on a flat surface and looking. Twist you cannot reliably see by eye, which is why winding sticks exist and why they're worth two minutes to make: two identical straight sticks, laid across the board at each end. Sight across the tops. If they're parallel, no twist. If one appears tilted relative to the other, that's your twist, magnified enough to actually see.

Two scraps of straight hardwood work fine. Paint the top edge of one dark and inlay a light strip if you want to get fancy, but plain sticks read well enough.

Method One: The Planer Sled

This is the workhorse. It's how most people without a jointer mill lumber, and it produces genuinely flat results.

The concept: a planer flattens the top surface relative to whatever the bottom is resting on. If the bottom rests on a rigid, flat sled and the board can't rock or flex, then the top comes out flat. Flip, plane the other side, and now both faces are flat and parallel.

Building it: a piece of ¾" MDF or plywood, a couple of inches wider than your planer's capacity minus a bit, and longer than your typical board. MDF is the better choice — it's flatter than most plywood and stays that way. Add a cleat at the trailing end so the board can't slide backward under the rollers, and keep the whole thing under your planer's minimum thickness capacity.

Using it — and this is the part that decides whether it works: place the board on the sled cup-side down, so it sits on its two high edges and rocks. Then shim every gap until it can't rock at all. Thin wedges, playing cards, folded paper — whatever fills the void. Hot glue the shims in place if they want to shift.

The failure mode is skipping the shimming, letting the rollers press the board down onto the sled, and getting your banana back. The shims are the whole method. Everything else is a piece of MDF.

Take light passes — a sixteenth or less. Once the top face is fully flat with no remaining low spots, flip the board directly onto the planer bed, no sled, and bring it to thickness.

Method Two: Hand Planes

Slower, quieter, no sled to build, and genuinely satisfying — right up until you're doing your ninth board and reconsidering your life choices.

The traditional sequence: a scrub plane or heavily-cambered jack worked diagonally across the grain to hog off the high spots fast, then a jointer plane along the length to true it, then a smoother if you want the surface finished rather than just flat.

You don't need three planes to start. A single No. 5 jack plane with a sharp iron does all three jobs adequately. It's the plane to own if you own one.

The method that makes this work is not brute force, it's measurement: winding sticks at both ends, a straightedge along the length and across the width, and pencil scribbles on the high spots so you can see what you've removed. Plane the scribbles off. Re-check. Repeat. You're removing material where the marks are, not planing the whole board evenly — that's the mistake that makes hand-flattening feel impossible.

Honest assessment: for one or two boards, this is faster than building a sled. For a stack of ten, it isn't close.

Method Three: The Router Sled

For anything too wide for your planer — slabs, wide panels, live-edge tops — this is the method.

Two parallel rails on either side of the workpiece, set dead level with each other. A carriage that rides on those rails holding a router with a wide flattening bit. You sweep the router across the width, advance a bit, sweep back, until the whole face has been machined to the plane defined by the rails.

The rails are everything. They have to be straight, and they have to be level with each other in both directions. Check with a long level and shim underneath. If the rails are off, the slab is off by exactly the same amount, and the router doesn't know or care.

Use a bottom-cleaning or surfacing bit — wide and flat-bottomed. Take shallow passes with substantial overlap between passes, maybe a third of the bit's width. It leaves faint ridges regardless; those sand out quickly.

It's slow, it makes an enormous amount of dust, and it's the only practical way to flatten a 20-inch slab in a home shop. Wear a respirator and don't do it inside without collection.

The One Everyone Suggests That Doesn't Work

"Just run it through the planer a few times, it'll flatten out."

No. Rollers press the bow flat, knives cut a uniform thickness, board springs back. You've made a thinner bowed board. Run it enough times and you get a very thin bowed board.

The only version of this that has any merit is with a very slight bow, a board short enough to be stiff, and light passes — the board's own rigidity resists the rollers enough that you get partial improvement. That's a narrow case, not a method.

Cheating, Honorably

Two shortcuts that are entirely legitimate:

Cut it shorter first. Bow and twist are proportional to length. An eight-foot board with a nasty twist becomes four two-foot boards with almost none. If your project needs short pieces anyway, rough-cut to length before milling and most of the problem disappears. This is the single best trick in this article and it costs nothing.

Rip it narrower. Same logic for cup. A 10-inch cupped board ripped into two 5-inch boards has a quarter of the cup in each. Mill them flat, glue them back together, and you've lost a saw kerf and gained a flat panel.

Both of these are what experienced people quietly do before they touch a machine. It's not avoiding the work. It's not creating the work in the first place.

Which One to Use

A few boards, already own a planer — planer sled. It's the default answer for a reason.

One board, and you like hand tools — planes. Genuinely faster for small numbers, and you don't have to build anything.

Anything wider than your planer — router sled. It's the only option.

Any of the above — cut it shorter and rip it narrower first. Always.

You don't need a jointer to mill lumber. You need a flat reference face, and there are three good ways to get one. The jointer is just the fastest.

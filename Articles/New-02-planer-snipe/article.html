Snipe is the shallow scoop your planer takes out of the first and last few inches of a board — usually two to four inches in, usually a few thousandths deep, always exactly where you didn't want it. Here's the thing that makes it maddening: the machine isn't broken. A planer with perfect snipe-free performance and a planer with an eighth-inch gouge on every board can be mechanically identical. The difference is almost never the machine. It's what's happening at the moment the board is held by only one roller instead of two.

What's Actually Happening

A lunchbox planer holds your board with two rubber feed rollers — one before the cutterhead, one after — and those rollers do more than pull. They press the board down against the bed, and that downward pressure is what keeps the cut depth honest.

Now walk a board through in your head. As it enters, only the infeed roller has it. There's nothing holding the trailing end down, so the board can pivot up slightly at the back, which tips the leading end down into the knives. Deeper cut. Snipe. Then the outfeed roller grabs it, both rollers are engaged, the board is pinned flat, and everything is fine for the whole middle of the board. Then the tail clears the infeed roller, only the outfeed roller has it, the board pivots again, and you get snipe on the trailing end too.

That's the entire mechanism. Snipe is a two-roller machine doing a one-roller job for about four inches at each end. Every fix below is a way of getting through those moments without letting the board move.

Fix One: Lift the Ends (Free, Works Immediately)

This is the technique that solves most snipe, costs nothing, and takes about a second per board. As the board enters, lift up gently on the trailing end. As it exits, lift gently on the leading end. You're counteracting the pivot with your hands.

Emphasis on gently. You're not lifting the board off the bed, you're removing the tendency to tip — think a couple of pounds of upward pressure, not a heave. Lift too hard and you'll cause snipe in the opposite direction, which is a genuinely impressive way to make the problem worse while feeling like you're helping.

If that alone fixes it, stop reading. A lot of people never need anything else.

Fix Two: Support the Board Properly

The reason hand-lifting works is that it's compensating for unsupported weight. Remove the unsupported weight and you don't need the hands.

Infeed and outfeed support needs to be dead level with the planer bed, or a hair above it — never below. That last part matters more than anything else in this article. A roller stand set a quarter inch low doesn't just fail to help, it actively causes snipe, because the board droops onto it and levers the other end up. A stand set a hair high does no harm at all. When in doubt, go high.

Check it with a straightedge across the planer bed and out onto the stand, not by eye. Eyes are terrible at this. A roller stand works fine, but honestly a shop-built table at the exact right height is better — it can't be knocked out of adjustment, and it doesn't skew the board sideways the way a single roller sometimes does.

Fix Three: Check the Bed Extensions

Nearly every lunchbox planer ships with folding infeed and outfeed tables, and nearly every one of them is adjustable. Also, nearly every one of them is set wrong out of the box, and almost nobody checks.

Those extensions should be level with the main bed or angled very slightly upward at the outer ends. There's usually an adjustment screw or a set of bolts underneath. Lay a straightedge from the main bed out to the end of each extension and look at the gap.

If the extension droops downward — which is the common failure, because gravity and years of use both pull that direction — every board you feed gets levered into the knives at exactly the wrong moment. Adjusting those two extensions is a ten-minute job that permanently fixes snipe on a lot of machines. It's the single most-skipped fix on this list.

Fix Four: The Sacrificial Board Trick

If you need genuinely snipe-free boards and you're done fiddling, this is the reliable answer: butt a scrap board of the same thickness against the end of your workpiece and feed them through nose-to-tail, touching. The snipe happens on the scrap. Your board never experiences a one-roller moment because there's always wood in both rollers.

You can run a whole stack this way — feed each board so it kisses the tail of the one ahead of it. First and last boards get sniped, everything between them comes out clean. When I'm milling a batch, I keep one ugly board specifically for this and it lives next to the planer.

The simple version of the advice is "leave your boards long and cut the snipe off." That works and you should do it anyway — always mill four to six inches longer than final length. But it's not really a fix, it's a tax. The sacrificial board is the actual fix.

Fix Five: Roller Pressure and Cutterhead Lock

Now we're into actual machine adjustment, and this is where you go only if the free stuff didn't work.

Feed roller tension is adjustable on most planers, usually via springs under the top cover. Too little pressure and the rollers can't hold the board down through the transitions. Too much and you get feed marks pressed into the surface and premature roller wear. This is a real adjustment with a real tradeoff — go a small amount at a time and test.

The cutterhead lock is the one people forget entirely. Many planers have a lever or knob that locks the cutterhead carriage against the four posts it rides on. If it's unlocked, the whole head can lift slightly as the board enters — which is snipe with a different cause and identical symptoms. If your planer has one and you've never touched it, go look. The DeWalt DW735's automatic head lock is a big part of why that machine has a reputation for minimal snipe.

Worn or glazed rollers lose grip. If yours look shiny and hard rather than matte rubber, clean them with mineral spirits and a rag. If that doesn't restore the texture, they're worn out, and replacing them is a real repair with real cost.

How to Tell Which One You've Got

Run a test board and look at where the snipe is:

Snipe only at the leading end — usually infeed support too low, or infeed extension drooping.

Snipe only at the trailing end — usually outfeed support too low, or outfeed extension drooping.

Both ends, roughly equal — normal single-roller behavior. Hand-lift or use the sacrificial board.

Both ends, deep and inconsistent — check the cutterhead lock first, then roller tension.

Snipe that got worse recently — something moved. Straightedge across the bed and extensions before you assume the machine is wearing out.

The Part Nobody Says Out Loud

A small amount of snipe is normal, and chasing zero is a way to spend a Saturday not building anything. Every lunchbox planer snipes a little. The manufacturers say so in the manual, quietly, near the back.

The working answer for almost everyone is: get the extensions and support level, mill your stock four to six inches long, and cut the ends off. That's not settling. That's what people who mill a lot of lumber actually do, and they don't think about snipe at all, because they designed it out of the process instead of out of the machine.

Level your supports. Lock your cutterhead. Mill long. Move on.

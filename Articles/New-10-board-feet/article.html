Walk into a hardwood dealer for the first time and the pricing makes no sense. There's no price on the board. There's a sign that says walnut is $12, and a stack of boards in wildly different sizes, and an expectation that you know what you're doing.

Hardwood is sold by board foot — a unit of volume, not length or area. Once that clicks, the whole system is straightforward. Until it clicks, you cannot compare two boards, budget a project, or tell whether you're being treated fairly.

The Math

A board foot is 144 cubic inches. Think of it as a piece 1 inch thick, 12 inches wide, and 12 inches long.

The formula, when length is in inches:

(Thickness × Width × Length) ÷ 144 = board feet

Or, when length is in feet, which is how boards are usually described:

(Thickness × Width × Length in feet) ÷ 12 = board feet

A 1" × 6" × 8' board: (1 × 6 × 8) ÷ 12 = 4 board feet. At $12/bf that's $48 for that board.

Worth doing a few by hand until the shape of it sticks, then stop — the Board Foot Calculator handles the arithmetic and the price-per-board-foot in one go, including a whole cut list at once.

Quarter Notation, Which Is the Part That Confuses Everyone

Rough hardwood thickness is written in quarters of an inch, with the mark after the number:

4/4 ("four quarter") = 1 inch
5/4 = 1¼ inch
6/4 = 1½ inch
8/4 = 2 inch
12/4 = 3 inch

It's just quarters. 4/4 is four quarters of an inch. Once you see it, you can't unsee it, and it stops looking like a code.

The important part: that measurement is the rough thickness, taken before surfacing. A 4/4 board is a nominal 1" rough. Surfaced on two sides it lands around ¾", sometimes 13/16". You are charged for the 4/4. You take home the ¾".

That's not a scam — you're paying for the wood the mill cut, and the material removed in surfacing had to come off. But it's the first place beginners get surprised, and it's why a project calculated at final dimensions comes up short.

How They Measure, and Where the Money Goes

Three conventions that all cost you a little:

Width is measured at the narrowest point. On a rough board with a wavy edge, they measure where it's skinniest. That one's in your favor, and it's about the only one that is.

Everything rounds up. A 7½" wide board is usually tallied as 8". Length rounds up to the next foot. Small amounts, but they accumulate across a stack.

Rough boards include material you'll never use. Wavy edges, checked ends, the outer inch of a live edge. You pay by the board foot for wood that becomes offcuts.

None of this is dishonest. It's the convention, it's applied to everyone, and it's why the second half of this article matters more than the formula.

The Waste Factor — the Number That Actually Matters

Here's the thing that separates a budget that works from one that doesn't: the board feet in your cut list is not the board feet you buy.

Between the two, you lose material to:

Surfacing. Rough to final thickness eats 15 to 25 percent of the volume. 4/4 rough to ¾" finished is a 25% loss before you make a single cut.

Defects. Knots, checks, splits, bark inclusions, the section that's a different color than the rest. Real, and unpredictable.

Cut waste. Every kerf, every trimmed end, every piece that's four inches too short to be useful.

Grain matching. If you care how a panel looks, you'll reject boards that would technically work. This is a real cost of caring about the result.

Practical multipliers on your finished board-foot total:

Simple project, straight cuts, S4S lumber: add 15–20%
Typical furniture from rough lumber: add 30–40%
Grain-matched panels, or figured wood: add 50%+
First time doing any of this: add 50%, seriously

Buying 40% extra feels wasteful standing at the till. Driving back for two more board feet of the same walnut from a different flitch, that doesn't match, three weeks later — that's the alternative, and it's worse.

What a Board Actually Costs You

Run it for real. A small table, 12 board feet of finished parts, in 4/4 walnut at $12/bf:

Finished need: 12 bf
Add 35% for surfacing and waste: 16.2 bf
Round up to what you can actually buy: 18 bf
At $12/bf: $216

Not $144, which is what the naive calculation says. The difference between those two numbers is the entire reason people are shocked at the lumberyard.

Rough Versus Surfaced, and When Each Is Right

S4S (surfaced four sides) is ready to use. Costs substantially more per board foot, and it's already been dimensioned by someone else — meaning you're locked into their thickness.

S2S (surfaced two sides) is faced but with rough edges. A middle ground; you joint an edge and go.

Rough is cheapest per board foot and requires a jointer and planer or the workarounds for not having one. You control the final thickness, which matters more than it sounds — being able to leave a tabletop at 7/8" instead of ¾" is a real design freedom.

The honest calculus: rough lumber saves roughly 30 to 40 percent, and costs you machine time and the machines themselves. If you build a lot, it's obviously worth it. If you build twice a year, paying the premium for S4S is entirely rational and nobody should make you feel otherwise.

Before You Go

Take a tape and a calculator. Or a phone. Tally as you pick, not at the register.

Know your minimum board size. If you need 30" parts, a 34" board is worthless to you at any price.

Ask about the minimum charge. Many yards have one, and it changes the math on a small purchase.

Look at the ends. End checks run further into the board than they appear. Assume you're losing 3 to 6 inches off each end of a rough board before you find sound wood.

Buy it all at once. Same flitch, same tree, same color. This is the one that people learn the hard way, and there's no fixing it after the fact.

The Short Version

Thickness × width × length ÷ 12, when length is in feet. Quarters are just quarters of an inch. You pay for rough dimensions and take home surfaced ones. Add 30 to 40 percent to your cut list before you buy, more if it's your first time or the grain has to match.

Do that and the lumberyard stops being intimidating. It's a volume calculation with a waste factor, and now you know both.

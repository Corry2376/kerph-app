If your cuts burn, if the offcut pinches, if you've got a saw that "pulls" and you've quietly learned to fight it — this is almost certainly your problem. A table saw has three things that must be parallel to each other: the miter slot, the blade, and the fence. When they aren't, the symptoms are cosmetic at best and dangerous at worst, and the fix is an hour with a dial indicator and a wrench.

The critical part, and the part that gets skipped: there's a required order. Miter slot first, then blade to slot, then fence to slot. Align them out of order and you'll do the work twice, because everything downstream references the thing you set before it.

Why the Order Is Non-Negotiable

The miter slot is your baseline. Not the blade, not the fence — the slot. It's machined into the cast iron table and it's the one surface you can't adjust, which is exactly what makes it the reference. Every sled, every miter gauge, every crosscut jig rides in that slot.

So: the blade gets aligned to the slot. Then the fence gets aligned to the slot. If you align the fence to the blade and then move the blade, you've undone the fence. Do it in order once and it stays done.

Before You Touch Anything

Unplug the saw. Not "switch it off" — unplug it. You're going to be reaching around an exposed blade with both hands and your attention on a dial indicator, and this is the single easiest safety decision in this article.

Then raise the blade fully, and check three things that will make your measurements lie if you skip them:

Arbor play. Grab the blade at 12 and 6 o'clock and try to rock it. Any perceptible play means worn arbor bearings, and no alignment will hold until that's fixed. Rare, but check.

Blade flatness. A warped blade makes every reading meaningless. Mark one tooth with a marker and use that same tooth for every measurement — this cancels out both blade runout and tooth-to-tooth variation. This trick is the difference between a real measurement and a fictional one.

A clean table. Pitch buildup under your gauge base throws readings. Wipe it down.

Step One: Blade Parallel to the Miter Slot

This is the alignment that matters most and the one that causes burning and kickback when it's off.

Put a dial indicator in the miter slot — either mounted to a miter gauge bar or in a purpose-made jig. A dial indicator setup runs about the cost of a decent blade and you will use it on every machine in the shop, not just this one.

Zero the indicator against your marked tooth at the front of the blade. Rotate the blade by hand so that same tooth travels to the back, slide the indicator back, and read it. That number is your error.

What's acceptable: under 0.003" across the blade is good. Under 0.005" is fine for most work. Over 0.010" and you're burning wood and you know it.

To adjust: On a contractor saw, the trunnions bolt to the underside of the table — loosen them slightly, tap the assembly, retighten, re-measure. On a cabinet saw, the trunnions mount to the cabinet and you shift the table instead. Either way it's four bolts, small taps with a dead-blow mallet, and patience. Loosen just enough to move under a firm tap; fully loose and the whole thing shifts on you.

Snug the bolts back down and measure again before you call it. Tightening moves things. That's not a defect, it's just how bolted cast iron behaves, and it's why "measure after tightening" is the whole game.

Step Two: Fence Parallel to the Miter Slot

Same reference, same method. Run the indicator along the fence face front to back, or use a reliable combination square measuring from the slot to the fence at both ends.

Here's where it gets interesting, and where you'll find genuine disagreement among people who know what they're doing. Some set the fence dead parallel. Others deliberately toe the outfeed end away from the blade by one to three thousandths.

The reasoning for the toe-out: it guarantees the workpiece can't get pinched between the fence and the back of the blade as it exits, which is a classic kickback setup. The reasoning against: a dead-parallel fence gives a marginally cleaner cut, and a properly functioning riving knife already prevents the pinch.

The simple answer is that either works. The real answer is that toe-out is the safer default for a saw you're still learning, and dead parallel is fine once your riving knife is always installed and you've built the habit of never freehanding a cut. What is never acceptable is toe-in — the outfeed end closer to the blade than the infeed end. That's the geometry that turns an offcut into a projectile.

Most fences adjust with two set screws on the head that let you rotate the fence body a few thousandths. Check your manual; they're all slightly different and they're all easy once you find them.

Step Three: Blade 90 Degrees to the Table

Different alignment, same afternoon. Raise the blade fully, set an accurate square against the plate — not the teeth, the teeth are set outward and will give you a false reading — and check.

Adjust with the bevel stop under the table, then set your 45-degree stop while you're there. Then, and this is the part people skip, check that your bevel gauge actually reads zero when the blade is square. The pointer is decorative until you calibrate it, and a gauge that reads 90 when the blade is at 89.4 will lie to you every time you use it.

Step Four: The Riving Knife or Splitter

The riving knife has to be in the same plane as the blade — not offset to one side. Sight along it, or lay a straightedge against the blade plate and see whether the knife tracks with it. An offset knife pushes the workpiece sideways into the blade, which is precisely the failure it exists to prevent.

It should also be slightly thinner than the kerf and no thicker than the blade plate. If you've changed to a thin-kerf blade, your factory riving knife may no longer be right for it. That's a real mismatch worth knowing about rather than discovering.

How to Tell What's Wrong from the Cut

Burning on the fence side, worse at the end of the cut — fence toed in, or blade out of parallel with the outfeed end closer.

Burning on the offcut side — blade out of parallel the other direction.

Cut that starts fine and pinches shut — blade-to-slot error, usually. Check that first regardless of what else you suspect.

Crosscuts out of square with a sled that used to be square — the sled is fine, the blade moved relative to the slot.

Bevel cuts off, 90-degree cuts fine — bevel stop, not alignment.

How Often, Honestly

Check alignment when you buy the saw, after you move it, after anything heavy gets dropped on the table, and once a year otherwise. Moving a saw is the big one — rolling a contractor saw across an uneven garage floor genuinely knocks trunnions around, and if your saw lives on a mobile base and gets shuffled weekly, check it more often than yearly.

An hour, once. Then every cut after that is square, doesn't burn, and doesn't try to throw anything at you. There aren't many hours in a shop that pay back like that one.
